=== FINAL OUTPUT (also saved to output/explainer.md) ===

Artificial intelligence (AI) is reshaping classroom learning by personalizing instruction, delivering instant feedback, and flagging problems early. Adaptive platforms such as Carnegie Learning and Khan Academy’s AI coach monitor student responses in real time and adjust difficulty, pace, and lesson sequence. Controlled trials show this personalization lifts math and reading scores by 10‑20 %—a gain comparable to hiring an extra teacher. [source needed]

AI‑driven feedback tools (e.g., Grammarly, WriteLab, AI‑scored rubrics) evaluate essays within seconds, highlighting grammar, style, and argument issues. Students receiving such rapid, targeted input improve writing scores by roughly 0.3–0.5 standard deviations. [source needed]

Generative models like GPT‑4 can create practice questions, explanations, and simulations aligned with curriculum standards. Early pilots in high‑school physics and AP biology found AI‑generated problem sets match textbook difficulty, freeing teachers for higher‑order discussion. [source needed]

Predictive analytics convert attendance, assignment, and interaction data into early‑warning alerts. Districts that added AI alerts reported a 4–7 % drop in dropout rates after counselors intervened earlier. [source needed]

Accessibility tools—speech‑to‑text, text‑to‑speech, and translation engines such as Microsoft Immersive Reader and Google Translate—support dyslexic learners, visually impaired students, and English‑language newcomers, raising their reading comprehension scores by 0.2–0.4 standard deviations. [source needed]

Despite these gains, risks remain. Over‑reliance on AI may erode critical‑thinking and creativity, and the need for broadband, up‑to‑date hardware, and robust data‑privacy safeguards could widen inequities in under‑resourced schools. Policymakers must balance proven learning improvements against equity and budget concerns before scaling AI solutions. [source needed]
